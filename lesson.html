Chapter 1 An allele that kills, at ten percent

Step 1. A disease with one wrong amino acid

In 1956 Vernon Ingram found the change. Beta globin, one of hemoglobin's chains, is 146 amino acids long, and in sickle hemoglobin exactly one differs. The sixth amino acid is valine instead of glutamate.

Normal hemoglobin is HbA and sickle hemoglobin is HbS. Each bead below is one amino acid, and the ring marks the only difference.

Glutamate carries a negative charge and likes water. Valine carries no charge and avoids water. That one swap leaves a sticky, water-avoiding patch on the outside of the protein. When HbS gives up its oxygen, the sticky patch of one molecule fits into a pocket on the next, and the molecules stack into long stiff fibers inside the cell.

The fibers bend the cell into the stiff crescent on the right. A normal cell folds through capillaries narrower than itself. A sickled one jams in small vessels and breaks apart early, causing pain, anemia and organ damage. Untreated, the disease is eventually fatal.

Step 2. Two copies, three kinds of people

Each person below is drawn as their two copies of chromosome 11. Blue carries HbA and gold carries HbS. In later examples gold marks the allele called q.

HbA/HbA people make only normal hemoglobin. HbS/HbS people have sickle cell disease. HbA/HbS people, who have sickle cell trait, make both kinds and are generally healthy.

So for the disease, HbS is recessive. Dominance is always about one trait. For a different trait, surviving malaria, the heterozygote will turn out to be the one that stands out.

Quick quiz

A village has 100 people. 80 are HbA/HbA, 20 are HbA/HbS and none are HbS/HbS. What is the frequency of the HbS allele?

There are 200 copies of the gene in 100 people. Only the 20 heterozygotes carry HbS, one copy each, so 20 of 200 copies are HbS. 20/200 = 0.10. The 0.20 is the genotype frequency of heterozygotes, not an allele frequency.

Step 3. The puzzle

Now the strange part. Without modern care, a child with sickle cell disease usually dies before having children, and two copies of HbS leave with them. Generation after generation that should drain HbS away, as it does most lethal recessive alleles.

It does not. In 1953 Anthony Allison found sickle cell trait common in the hot, wet lowlands of East Africa and rare in highlands and dry regions. In 2015 Eric Elguero and colleagues tested 3,959 people across Gabon and found an HbS frequency of 0.105, the number your Session 24 handout uses.

So here is the one question this page answers. How can an allele that kills almost everyone who carries two copies stay near 10 percent, generation after generation, and can you predict the number?

Quick quiz

Allison found sickle trait common in hot wet lowlands and rare in highlands. What is the most natural first hypothesis?

When an allele's frequency tracks an environment, the first suspect is that the environment changes its fitness. Allison's suspect was malaria, which is transmitted by mosquitoes that thrive in hot wet lowlands.

Chapter 2 Darwin's argument, in three sentences

Step 4. Three premises and one inference

Lecture 23 slide 5 gives Darwin's argument as three premises and one conclusion, each something you can check in a real population.

  • Premise 1. In all organisms, more offspring are produced than survive and reproduce.
  • Premise 2. Organisms differ in their ability to survive and reproduce. Not all of the losses are random.
  • Premise 3. Some of these differences are heritable. Parents pass them to their offspring.

Put the three together and a conclusion falls out with no extra assumption. In every generation, the types that survive and reproduce better will make up a bigger share of the next generation than they did of this one. Their frequency rises. That inference is natural selection.

Step 5. Three requirements, checked one at a time

Session 24 turns the premises into three requirements you can test for any trait. The trait must vary in the population. The trait must be heritable. The trait must affect fitness. If any one is missing, selection cannot change that trait.

Human height passes all three. It varies, it is heritable, and there is some evidence that taller men have more children.

A scar from an accident varies but is not heritable, so selecting on scars changes nothing next generation. A neutral DNA letter varies and is heritable but does not affect fitness, so it only drifts.

Quick quiz

A gardener cuts the tallest weeds every week, so short weeds set more seed. But in this weed species height depends only on soil, not genes. What happens to the average height of the next generation grown in the same soil?

Requirement 2 fails. The survivors differed in height, but their seeds carry no genetic tendency to be short. Selection on a non-heritable difference produces no response.

Step 6. Hardy-Weinberg is where you start, not where nature is

Hardy-Weinberg is the null model. Lecture 23 slide 2 lists five ways real populations break it, new mutations, finite populations, migration, non-random mating and natural selection.

Slide 3 calls selection the most important, because it is often strong, often pushes the same way every generation, and improves the fit of a population to its environment. Drift has no direction, migration only mixes, and mutation is too slow. Only selection builds adaptation.

Chapter 3 Fitness is a number you can count

Step 7. Two parts, multiplied

To calculate selection, the ability to survive and reproduce has to become a number, fitness. Lecture 23 slide 9 splits it into two parts.

The first part is viability. That is the chance that a newly formed individual, a zygote, survives to breeding age. The second part is fecundity, the number of offspring a survivor produces over its lifetime. Fitness is viability times fecundity.

Why times, and not plus? Ten zygotes, two survive, and each survivor has 30 offspring. The ten leave 60, which is 6 per zygote, and 0.2 × 30 = 6. The product is offspring per individual born, the absolute fitness w.

The figure draws slide 10's example. Each dot is one zygote split into its two allele copies, and a hollow dot with an X died before breeding.

Read the bottom row. Nine of ten aa zygotes died and the survivor laid 10 eggs, so ten aa zygotes leave 10 offspring and w = 1. AA leaves six times as many. Your study guide calls w the raw growth rate of a genotype.

Common mix-up

Fitness is not strength, size or how long you live. A genotype that lives long and has no offspring has fitness zero. A genotype that dies young after laying a thousand eggs can have enormous fitness. Fitness only counts offspring left in the next generation.

Quick quiz

A genotype has viability 0.5 and fecundity 8. What is its absolute fitness?

Fitness is viability times fecundity. 0.5 × 8 = 4. Half the zygotes survive and each survivor leaves 8, so each zygote leaves 4 on average.

Quick quiz

Harder. AA is the most fit genotype, with absolute fitness 8. Aa has viability 0.8 and a selection coefficient of 0.25. What is the fecundity of Aa?

Work backward one link at a time. s = 0.25 means W = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75. Relative fitness is w ÷ wmax, so w = 0.75 × 8 = 6. Absolute fitness is viability times fecundity, so fecundity = 6 ÷ 0.8 = 7.5. The 6 is the absolute fitness itself, one step short. The 2.5 uses s where W belongs, 0.25 × 8 = 2, then 2 ÷ 0.8.

Step 8. Measuring only part of fitness

Real studies rarely measure the whole life cycle. Slide 9 says we sometimes measure just part of fitness and assume the other parts are not selected.

Session 23 question 1D shows why that can work. If every finch survivor had exactly 5 offspring, every genotype's share would be multiplied by 5, so the proportions, and so the allele frequencies, would not change.

So one component is enough only if the others are equal across genotypes. In the eggs chapter you will see what happens when they are not.

Quick quiz

Researchers measure only survival to adulthood. When is the predicted change in allele frequency still correct?

An equal fecundity multiplies every fitness and the mean fitness by the same number, so it cancels. Session 23 question 1D is exactly this point.

Step 9. Mean fitness, the average across the population

The mean fitness of the population, written W̄ and read "W bar," is the average fitness of an individual. Multiply each genotype's fitness by how common it is, then add.

W̄ = freq(AA)·wAA + freq(Aa)·wAa + freq(aa)·waa

In Hardy-Weinberg proportions that is W̄ = p²wAA + 2pqwAa + q²waa. With real genotype counts, use the counted frequencies instead.

Try it with slide 11's numbers. The frequency of A is p = 0.1 and of a is q = 0.9. The fitnesses are 6, 4 and 1.

p² = 0.01, and 0.01 × 6 = 0.06. Then 2pq = 0.18, and 0.18 × 4 = 0.72. Last, q² = 0.81, and 0.81 × 1 = 0.81. Add them. 0.06 + 0.72 + 0.81 = 1.59. On average an individual in this population leaves 1.59 offspring.

Quick quiz

p = 0.5 and fitnesses of AA, Aa, aa are 6, 4 and 1. Assuming Hardy-Weinberg, what is W̄?

p² = q² = 0.25 and 2pq = 0.5. So W̄ = 0.25(6) + 0.5(4) + 0.25(1) = 1.5 + 2 + 0.25 = 3.75. The 3.67 is the unweighted average of 6, 4 and 1, which ignores how common each genotype is.

A population has genotype frequencies 0.2 AA, 0.2 Aa and 0.6 aa, counted directly. Fitnesses are 1, 1, 0.5. What is W̄?

Use the counted frequencies, not Hardy-Weinberg ones. 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.6(0.5) = 0.70. The 0.755 comes from computing q = 0.7 and then wrongly using p², 2pq and q², which are not the real frequencies here.

Chapter 4 One dry season on Daphne Major

Step 10. Darwin never saw it happen

Darwin thought selection was too slow to watch. From 1973 Peter and Rosemary Grant followed the medium ground finches on Daphne Major in the Galápagos, measuring beaks, recording who lived and bred, and keeping blood. Small beaks handle small seeds and large beaks open large ones.

In 2004 and 2005 a severe drought hit, and a bigger species, the large ground finch, ate nearly all the large seeds. Medium ground finches with large beaks were left with food they were worst at, and many died.

In 2016 Sangeet Lamichhaney and the Grants found a beak-size gene, HMGA2, and genotyped the stored blood of birds that survived and birds that died. Call its alleles S, for small beaks, and L, for large.

Step 11. Seventy-one birds

Here are the Session 23 question 1A data, one dot per bird, split into its two HMGA2 copies. Blue is S and gold is L, the allele selected against, so its frequency is q. Crossed hollow dots died.

14 of 19 SS birds lived, 17 of 32 SL and only 6 of 20 LL. Birds with more L copies died more, so L will fall. The question is by how much.

First the starting point. LL birds carry two L and SL birds one, so there are 2 × 20 + 32 = 72 L out of 142 copies, and q = 0.507. The key gets the same from frequencies, 0.282 + 0.451/2 = 0.507.

Counts or p², 2pq, q²? The one question that decides it

Ask who selection acts on.

  • Given only q and told Hardy-Weinberg, as in the lecture example, the wolves, CCR5 and lactase. Use p², 2pq and q².
  • Given counts, and selection acts on those very individuals, as with the finches here. Use the counted frequencies, whatever they are. This is also the default when a problem gives genotype frequencies and does not say.
  • Given counted parents, and selection acts on their offspring. The offspring are new zygotes from random mating, so build them from the parents' q as p², 2pq and q². A question that says calculate p², 2pq and q² is telling you this is the case.
  • Given only phenotype counts with a dominant allele. You cannot count alleles, so assume Hardy-Weinberg and take q from the recessive homozygotes.

Two small errors on slide 14 of the key

The key writes 0.286 + 0.451/2 = 0.511. The LL frequency is 20/71 = 0.282, not 0.286, so the starting frequency of L is 0.507. The change is then 0.392 − 0.507 = −0.115, not −0.119. If you are asked for the change, −0.115 is the correct value.

Quick quiz

Why can you not get q by taking the square root of the LL frequency, √0.282?

√freq(aa) = q only if freq(aa) = q², which is a Hardy-Weinberg assumption. With real counts you always count alleles directly. Study Guide 12 makes this exact point in its notes.

Step 13. The table is just counting the survivors

Now look at what the rows did physically. The drought removed 34 of 71 birds. Among the 37 survivors there are 2 × 6 + 17 = 29 L out of 74 copies, and 29/74 = 0.392, exactly the table's answer.

When fitness is survival, selection is literally remove the dead and count again. The table does that with frequencies, so it also works with fecundity or when you only know proportions. Written as one line, it is Lecture 23 slide 13's general equation #1.

q′ = ½·freq(Aa)·WAa + freq(aa)·WaaW̄

The top counts every a in aa individuals and half the alleles in Aa individuals, each weighted by fitness. The bottom, W̄, rescales so the frequencies add to 1. The prime means next generation.

Session 23 question 1B asks about the ½. The key says the numerator counts all alleles in the homozygotes but only half the alleles in the heterozygotes. Write that.

For p′ use the two genotypes that contain A, as Problem Set 12 question 4 says.

p′ = ½·freq(Aa)·WAa + freq(AA)·WAAW̄

Quick quiz

In general equation #1 for q′, why is the heterozygote term multiplied by ½?

The numerator counts a alleles. Every aa individual's copies are all a, but only half of each Aa individual's copies are a. The ½ has nothing to do with fitness.

Step 14. How big is a change of 0.115?

The frequency of L fell from 0.507 to 0.392 in one season. The key calls it "a HUGE change in one generation." It is.

Session 23 draws two lessons. Change can be fast in real events, and the selecting environment can be physical, the drought, or biological, the competitor that ate the big seeds.

Chapter 5 Your turn. Add the eggs

Step 15. The survivors did not all breed equally

Session 23 question 2 asks a what-if. Suppose the surviving large-beaked birds had the big seeds to themselves and raised more chicks. The handout invents fecundities of 3 for SS, 4 for SL and 7 for LL, with the same survival as before.

Now fitness has both parts. Do each step below with your calculator, three decimals.

Quick quiz

Part 1. What is the fitness of SS, now including fecundity?

Fitness is viability times fecundity. 0.737 × 3 = 2.21. The 3.74 adds them, which is never right.

What are the fitnesses of SL and LL?

SL is 0.531 × 4 = 2.125, which the key writes 2.12. LL is 0.300 × 7 = 2.10. Look at how close the three fitnesses now are. The small-beaked birds survive better but the large-beaked ones breed more.

Part 2. The genotype frequencies are still 0.268, 0.451 and 0.282. What is the mean fitness?

0.268 × 2.21 = 0.592, 0.451 × 2.125 = 0.958, and 0.282 × 2.10 = 0.592. Add them. 2.14. The 6.43 is the sum of the three fitnesses, forgetting to weight them by frequency.

Part 3. What is the frequency of SL after selection?

Divide its contribution by the mean. 0.958/2.141 = 0.447, exactly 68/152. Its share barely moved because its fitness, 2.125, is so close to the mean, 2.14.

Part 4. SS and LL after selection are both 0.276. What is the new frequency of L?

q′ = freq(LL) + ½ freq(SL) = 0.276 + 0.447/2 = 0.500. The S allele comes out at 0.500 too.

The frequency of L started at 0.507. Including fecundity, what is Δq?

0.500 − 0.507 = −0.007. Leaving out fecundity had made it look like a huge change. Including it, the two components nearly cancel.

Step 16. Why it came out exactly even

That 0.500 is not a rounding accident. Count offspring the way you counted survivors. The 14 SS survivors had 3 chicks each, 42 chicks. The 17 SL survivors had 4 each, 68 chicks. The 6 LL survivors had 7 each, 42 chicks.

Now count the alleles these parents put into those chicks. Each chick gets one allele from this parent. The SS parents give 42 S. The SL parents give S to half their chicks and L to the other half, 34 and 34. The LL parents give 42 L. That is 42 + 34 = 76 S and 34 + 42 = 76 L. Exactly equal, so the new frequency is exactly one half. Equation #1 does the same count per bird. Its top, ½ × 0.958 + 0.592 = 1.071, is half the SL chicks plus all the LL chicks, per bird before the drought. Its bottom, W̄ = 2.141, is all the chicks per bird. 1.071 ÷ 2.141 = 0.500, the same as 76 ÷ 152.

That is why fitness has to cover the whole life cycle. Survival favored S and breeding favored L, so measuring only one would predict a big change the whole life cycle does not produce. These fecundities were invented, so the point is the method, not the history.

Quick quiz

Harder. Before a flood a population has 40 AA, 40 Aa and 20 aa. Afterwards 30 AA, 28 Aa and 6 aa are alive. Each AA survivor has 2 offspring, each Aa 3 and each aa 6. What is the frequency of a among the alleles these parents pass on?

Count what the parents pass on, as in Step 18. AA parents have 30 × 2 = 60 offspring, each given A. Aa parents have 28 × 3 = 84, half given a, so 42 a and 42 A. aa parents have 6 × 6 = 36, all given a. That is 42 + 36 = 78 a out of 60 + 84 + 36 = 180, so 78 ÷ 180 = 0.433. Before the flood a was (2 × 20 + 40) ÷ 200 = 0.400, so it rose. The 0.313 counts survivors only, (2 × 6 + 28) ÷ 128, and says a fell. The 0.563 uses fecundity only, 180 a out of 320, and overstates the rise. Survival and fecundity pull opposite ways, and only both together give the answer.

Chapter 6 Only comparisons matter

Step 17. Mean fitness is the yardstick

You have now seen it three ways. Multiplying every fitness by 5 changed nothing, and doubling every fecundity changed nothing. What matters is never how big a fitness is but how big it is compared with the others, as a ratio. Multiply every fitness by the same number and nothing changes. Add the same number to every fitness and selection gets weaker, because 0.5 out of 1 is a big shortfall and 10.5 out of 11 is a small one.

Dividing by W̄ is what makes that true. Session 23 question 1C asks why mean fitness is in the equation. The key says it rescales the absolute fitnesses, and what counts is how much they differ from the mean fitness, meaning as a fraction of the mean.

Quick quiz

Harder. Population X has fitnesses 1, 1 and 0.5 for AA, Aa and aa. Population Y has fitnesses 11, 11 and 10.5. Both are at q = 0.5 under Hardy-Weinberg. Which is true?

Divide by the largest fitness. X becomes 1, 1 and 0.5, so s = 0.5. Y becomes 1, 1 and 10.5 ÷ 11 = 0.955, so s = 0.045. For X, top −0.5 × 0.5 × 0.25 = −0.0625, bottom 1 − 0.5 × 0.25 = 0.875, Δq = −0.071. For Y, top −0.045 × 0.5 × 0.25 = −0.0057, bottom 1 − 0.045 × 0.25 = 0.989, Δq = −0.0057. Multiplying every fitness by a number changes nothing, but adding a number to every fitness weakens selection. What matters is each fitness as a fraction of the others, not the gap in absolute units.

Step 18. Relative fitness W and the selection coefficient s

Lecture 24 slide 6 gives a standard way to write the comparison. Divide every absolute fitness w by the largest, wmax. That is relative fitness W, so the best genotype has W = 1. The selection coefficient s says how much worse a genotype is than the best.

W = w ÷ wmax

s = 1 − W, and so W = 1 − s

s runs from 0 to 1, where s = 1 is completely lethal. The reference genotype is the most fit one everywhere in this unit except heterozygote disadvantage, where Problem Set 12 question 9 uses the heterozygote instead.

Slide 7's green foliage beetles. Recessive brown beetles stand out on green leaves and get eaten, w = 1.2, 1.2 and 0.92. Divide by 1.2. W = 1, 1 and 0.77, so s = 0, 0 and 0.23.

Quick quiz

Absolute fitnesses are 10, 8 and 4. What are the relative fitnesses?

Divide by the largest, 10. The 0.45, 0.36, 0.18 divides by the sum, which is a proportion, not a relative fitness. The 0, 0.2, 0.6 are the selection coefficients.

Step 19. Your turn. Run the lecture example

Now run Lecture 23 slide 11. p = 0.1, q = 0.9, fitnesses 6, 4 and 1, Hardy-Weinberg, W̄ = 1.59.

Quick quiz

Normalize by W̄. What is the frequency of Aa after selection?

0.72/1.59 = 0.453. Before selection Aa was 0.18. The heterozygotes more than doubled their share because their fitness, 4, is far above the mean, 1.59.

Step 20. After selection the genotypes are not p², 2pq, q²

A mistake that costs points every year. In the problem you just did, Aa after selection is 0.453. But 2p′q′ = 2 × 0.264 × 0.736 = 0.389. They do not match.

Selection removed some genotypes more than others after the zygotes formed, so the survivors are not a random pairing of alleles. Hardy-Weinberg returns only after they mate at random.

So after selection, the heterozygote frequency is 2pq times its fitness divided by W̄, and an allele frequency is the homozygote plus half the heterozygote. Never use 2p′q′ or a square root.

freq(Aa) after selection = 2pq·WAa ÷ W̄

A missing 2 in the study guide

Study Guide 12 writes the heterozygote frequency after selection as pqWAa/W̄. It should be 2pqWAa/W̄. Check it on the slide example. 2(0.1)(0.9)(4)/1.59 = 0.453, which matches the table. Without the 2 you get 0.226, which is the heterozygotes' contribution to each allele, not their frequency.

Quick quiz

After one round of selection, q′ = 0.736. A student says the aa frequency is now 0.736² = 0.542. What is wrong?

Selection changed genotype frequencies directly. The aa frequency among survivors is 0.81/1.59 = 0.509. Only after random mating would aa become q′² in the next zygotes.

Step 21. Mean fitness climbs

Slide 12 recomputes W̄ with the new allele frequencies and gets about 2.49, up from 1.59. The population got fitter.

That uses p′², 2p′q′ and q′², which is allowed here because this is the mean fitness of the next generation's zygotes, after the survivors mate at random. When a question asks for mean fitness after selection, it means this value, not the average among the survivors.

Quick quiz

Harder. Hardy-Weinberg, q = 0.4, and W = 1, 0.8 and 0.5 for AA, Aa and aa. What is W̄ in the next generation?

First this generation. W̄ = 0.36(1) + 0.48(0.8) + 0.16(0.5) = 0.36 + 0.384 + 0.08 = 0.824. Then q′ = (0.24 × 0.8 + 0.16 × 0.5) ÷ 0.824 = (0.192 + 0.08) ÷ 0.824 = 0.330, so p′ = 0.670. The next generation's zygotes are back in Hardy-Weinberg, so W̄ = 0.670² + 2(0.670)(0.330)(0.8) + 0.330²(0.5) = 0.449 + 0.354 + 0.054 = 0.857. The 0.824 is this generation's value. The 0.767 is the unweighted average of 1, 0.8 and 0.5.

Chapter 7 The fitness an allele experiences

Step 22. Follow one allele instead of three genotypes

So far you followed genotypes. Now ask the question that decides an allele's fate. If you are one copy of a, how well do you do on average?

A copy of a always sits next to a second copy. Under random mating that partner is another a with probability q, making aa, or an A with probability p, making Aa.

So the fitness an a allele experiences is q·waa + p·wAa, the two genotypes it can be in, weighted by how often it is in each.

Step 23. Average excess, a number with a sign

Lecture 23 slide 15 calls the difference between that and W̄ the average excess of a. This page writes it αa so it is never mistaken for the genotype aa.

αa = q·waa + p·wAa − W̄

If a alleles do better than the average allele, αa is positive. If worse, negative.

And then the change in frequency is that excess, divided by W̄ to turn it into a fraction of the average, and multiplied by q, how common the allele is.

Δq = qW̄ · αa

That is general equation #2. It follows from equation #1 in one line. Put in Hardy-Weinberg frequencies. The heterozygote term is ½ × 2pq × wAa = pq·wAa, so q′ = (pq·wAa + q²·waa) ÷ W̄. Pull a q out of the top. q′ = q(p·wAa + q·waa) ÷ W̄. Subtract q, written as q·W̄ ÷ W̄, and you get Δq = q(p·wAa + q·waa − W̄) ÷ W̄. The bracket is αa.

Step 24. Check it on the slide example

Check it on slide 11's numbers. An a allele sits with another a 90 percent of the time and with A 10 percent, so it experiences 0.9 × 1 + 0.1 × 4 = 1.3. The population average is 1.59, so αa = −0.29.

Then Δq = (0.9 ÷ 1.59) × (−0.29) = −0.164, exactly the table's answer.

For A, αA = 0.1 × 6 + 0.9 × 4 − 1.59 = 2.61, and Δp = +0.164. Notice p·αA + q·αa = 0.261 − 0.261 = 0. Weighted by frequency the two excesses always cancel, because together the alleles are the population.

Quick quiz

q = 0.5 and fitnesses are 6, 4, 1, with Hardy-Weinberg frequencies. What is the average excess of a?

W̄ = 0.25(6) + 0.5(4) + 0.25(1) = 3.75. The a allele experiences 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 4 = 2.5. So αa = 2.5 − 3.75 = −1.25.

Using that, what is Δq?

Δq = (q/W̄)·αa = (0.5/3.75)(−1.25) = −0.167. Check with equation #1. q′ = (0.25 × 4 + 0.25 × 1)/3.75 = 0.333, and 0.333 − 0.5 = −0.167.

Quick quiz

Harder. Under Hardy-Weinberg, q = 0.4, W̄ = 0.9 and Δq = −0.02. What is the average excess of a?

Δq = (q ÷ W̄)·αa, so αa = Δq × W̄ ÷ q = −0.02 × 0.9 ÷ 0.4 = −0.045. The −0.050 is −0.02 ÷ 0.4, which forgets W̄. The −0.0072 multiplies everything instead of undoing the division.

Step 25. The sign is the whole story

Slide 17's conclusion. In Δq = (q/W̄)·αa, q and W̄ are always positive, so the direction of change depends only on the sign of the average excess.

An allele that does better than the average allele increases, and one that does worse decreases. As fitter alleles spread, W̄ climbs.

Quick quiz

Can an allele that is lethal as a homozygote have a positive average excess?

Average excess depends on where the allele usually is. A rare HbS allele is almost always in a heterozygote, and in malarial regions heterozygotes are the fittest genotype.

Quick quiz

Harder. W = 0.7, 1 and 0 for AA, Aa and aa, so aa is lethal. Hardy-Weinberg, q = 0.1. What is Δq?

W̄ = 0.81(0.7) + 0.18(1) + 0.01(0) = 0.567 + 0.18 = 0.747. An a copy sits with a with probability 0.1 (fitness 0) and with A with probability 0.9 (fitness 1), so it experiences 0.9. αa = 0.9 − 0.747 = +0.153. Δq = (0.1 ÷ 0.747)(0.153) = +0.0205. A lethal homozygote does not make the allele fall when the allele is rare, because almost every copy sits in the fittest genotype. The +0.153 forgets to multiply by q ÷ W̄.

Step 26. When you may not use average excess

Slide 15 begins "Assuming Hardy-Weinberg genotype frequencies." The partner of an a allele was drawn with probabilities q and p, which is only true under random mating.

The Session 23 key says it about the finches. "You can't use our average excess equation." Their frequencies were counted, not p², 2pq and q². General equation #1 is the only tool that works with any genotype frequencies. Equation #2 and every special model need Hardy-Weinberg.

Chapter 8 Six names for selection

Step 27. Three ways selection can act on a trait

Exam questions describe a situation and ask which type of selection it is. There are two sets of three names. One describes a trait and the other describes an allele.

For a trait like beak size, plot how many individuals have each value and you get a hump. In the figure dashed gray is before selection and solid is after.

Directional, either higher or lower values are favored and the hump slides one way, like long-necked giraffes. Stabilizing, central values are favored and the hump narrows, like gray mice on gray rock. Disruptive, both extremes are favored and the hump can split in two, like light and dark moths.

Step 28. Reading a real fitness curve

Session 23 question 3A shows Hendry and colleagues' finch data from 2005. The beak histogram has two humps, and the fitness curve has two peaks, a higher one at large beaks, with a dip between.

The height of the fitness line is how well birds of that size did. Two peaks and a valley mean both extremes beat the middle, which is disruptive selection.

Step 29. Your turn. Name the trait selection

Decide whether each case is directional, stabilizing or disruptive.

Case 1. Human birth weight. Very small and very large babies have the highest death rates, and average babies the lowest.

The middle is favored and both tails are cut. That is the textbook case of stabilizing selection. The mean stays put and the spread shrinks.

Case 2. During the 2004 to 2005 drought, large-beaked medium ground finches lost the big seeds to a larger species and died more often than small-beaked ones.

Only one end, the large end, was hit. The whole distribution slides toward small beaks. Directional.

Case 3. Hendry and colleagues found that medium ground finches with small beaks and with large beaks both had higher fitness than birds in between.

Both extremes beat the middle. That is disruptive selection, and it is exactly the Session 23 question 3A graph.

Case 4. Giraffe ancestors with longer necks reached more leaves and left more offspring than those with short necks.

One extreme, long, is favored. This is the slide 7 cartoon for directional selection.

Case 5. Mice of intermediate gray color are best camouflaged on a gray rock field. White and dark mice are eaten more.

An intermediate value is favored and both extremes are removed. Stabilizing, the middle panel of slide 7.

Case 6. Moths on a patchwork of pale lichen and dark soot. Very light and very dark moths both hide well, but intermediate moths stand out everywhere.

Quick quiz

Harder. A fitness curve for beak depth is a single hill peaking at 12 mm. The population's beaks average 9 mm, and almost none are above 11 mm. What is selection doing right now?

Harder. Mean beak size on an island has not changed in 20 years. A student concludes that no selection acts on beak size. What is the best response?

Step 30. Three ways selection can act on an allele

The second set describes one allele's frequency over time, the three curves of Lecture 23 slide 8.

Positive selection is a favored new mutation rising from rare to near 1, slowly at first and then fast. That rise is a selective sweep, like the peppered moth or Lenski's E. coli.

Negative, or purifying, selection removes new harmful mutations, so the curve stays pinned near zero. It keeps genes from degrading by mutation and drift.

Balancing selection holds both alleles at an equilibrium. The main mechanism in this course is heterozygote advantage. Slide 4 lists sickle cell, PKU and CFTR.

Keep the two sets apart

Directional, stabilizing and disruptive describe a trait's distribution. Positive, negative and balancing describe an allele's frequency. Problem Set 12 question 5 asks you to say which kind each one applies to, so always add "on a trait" or "on genes." The same event can be described both ways. Selection for larger size is directional on the trait and positive on each allele that increases size.

Chapter 9 Three shortcuts, and the rule for q

Step 32. Why special models exist

General equation #1 always works. But with Hardy-Weinberg proportions and a simple fitness pattern it collapses into a shorter formula. Lecture 24 slide 5 lists three cases. Selection against the recessive, selection against the dominant, and heterozygote superiority.

Step 33. The one rule. q is the allele selected against

In the last unit q was always the recessive allele. Here, as Study Guide 12 warns, q is the allele selected against, not the recessive one.

In heterozygote advantage both homozygotes are hurt, so each gets its own coefficient. s1 goes with the p homozygote and s2 with the q homozygote. Chapter 11 builds it.

Quick quiz

You call the favored allele q and get Δq = −0.02 from a special formula. What went wrong?

The minus sign in front of every special Δq means q is the allele being removed. The Session 24 key uses exactly this check.

Step 34. Selection against the recessive, built from scratch

Green foliage beetles. Only aa is hurt, so the relative fitnesses are 1, 1 and 1 − s, and q is the recessive a.

Mean fitness is W̄ = p² + 2pq + q²(1 − s) = 1 − sq². The population loses fitness only through aa, a fraction q² of it.

General equation #1 gives q′ = [pq + q²(1 − s)] ÷ W̄, and pq + q² = q, so q′ = (q − sq²) ÷ (1 − sq²).

Subtract q. Δq = −sq²(1 − q) ÷ (1 − sq²), and 1 − q = p. That is Lecture 24 slide 9's formula.

Δq = −spq²1 − sq² selection against the recessive

The q² on top is the key. Selection only touches a alleles in aa homozygotes, a fraction q² of the population.

Step 35. Selection against the dominant, built from scratch

Brown foliage beetles. AA and Aa are green and get eaten, aa is brown and safe. A is selected against and dominant, so A is q and a is p. Write that down first.

AA, Aa and aa have frequencies q², 2pq and p² and fitnesses 1 − s, 1 − s and 1. W̄ = 1 − s(q² + 2pq) = 1 − 2sq + sq², the slide's denominator.

Copies of A sit in AA and Aa, both at 1 − s, so q′ = q(1 − s) ÷ W̄. Subtracting q gives Δq = q(−s + 2sq − sq²) ÷ W̄ = −sq(1 − q)² ÷ W̄, and 1 − q = p.

Δq = −sp²q1 − 2sq + sq² selection against the dominant

Compare the tops. Against the recessive it has q², against the dominant just q. Every copy of a dominant harmful allele is exposed.

Quick quiz

A dominant allele is lethal, s = 1, and q = 0.2. What is q after one generation?

With s = 1, every individual carrying even one copy dies before breeding. Δq = −p²q/(1 − 2q + q²) = −p²q/p² = −q. The allele is gone in one generation, unless new mutations bring it back.

Green foliage beetles have W = 1, 1, 0.77 and q = 0.3. What is Δq?

s = 0.23. Top is −0.23 × 0.7 × 0.09 = −0.01449. Bottom is 1 − 0.23 × 0.09 = 0.9793. Δq = −0.01449/0.9793 = −0.0148. The −0.0207 is just −sq², which leaves out p and the denominator. Always compute top and bottom separately.

Worked example. Run it backwards

Suppose a dominant harmful allele fell from 0.5 to 0.4 in one generation. What was s? Write what you know. Δq = −0.1, q = 0.5 and p = 0.5. Leave s as the unknown in the formula. The top is −s × 0.25 × 0.5 = −0.125s. The bottom is 1 − 2s(0.5) + s(0.25) = 1 − 0.75s. Set top over bottom equal to −0.1 and multiply both sides by the bottom. −0.125s = −0.1 + 0.075s, so 0.2s = 0.1 and s = 0.5. Check it forward. −0.0625 ÷ 0.625 = −0.1. Reverse problems are the same formula with a different blank, and the bottom is the part people forget.

Step 36. Why a rare recessive is so hard to remove

Lecture 24 slide 10 says purifying selection against rare mutants is harder for recessives. A harmful recessive is only hurt in a homozygote. In a heterozygote it is masked.

Under Hardy-Weinberg a fraction q of all a copies sit in homozygotes, because each copy's partner is another a with probability q. At q = 0.5 that is half, at q = 0.01 one in a hundred. In the toggle, filled dots are in aa, where selection sees them, and hollow dots hide in heterozygotes.

Half of all a alleles are in aa individuals. Selection against aa sees half of them, so a common recessive allele drops fast.

Now only 10 percent of a alleles are in aa individuals. The other 90 percent ride in heterozygotes that look and survive exactly like AA.

One in a hundred. Ninety nine percent of the a alleles are invisible to selection. This is why a rare harmful recessive lingers for thousands of generations.

Step 43. Allison's suspicion

Back to the puzzle. Allison's 1953 surveys found sickle trait common where malaria was intense, and in 1954 he showed that children with the trait carried fewer and lighter malaria infections.

Step 45. Where the two pushes cancel

An allele rises if the fitness it experiences beats W̄. W̄ is the frequency-weighted average of what the two alleles experience, so if one experienced more than the other it would be above W̄ and rise. At equilibrium neither rises, so both experience the same fitness.

With q = freq(HbS), an HbS allele sits with HbA, fitness 1, with probability p, and with HbS, fitness 0, with probability q. It experiences p = 1 − q.

An HbA allele experiences 0.879p + q.

The figure plots both. Gold is HbS and blue is HbA, and the ring marks where they cross.

Left of the crossing HbS does better and rises. Right of it HbS does worse and falls. Every path leads to the crossing, a stable equilibrium.

An A copy experiences p(1 + s1) + q and an a copy q(1 + s2) + p. Setting them equal again gives p·s1 = q·s2, so the balance point is still s1 ÷ (s1 + s2). But now it is unstable. Start on either side and the allele runs away to 0 or 1.

The point is not always 0.5, so "the rarer allele loses" is not the rule. With AA 1.3, Aa 1 and aa 1.1 the point is 0.3/0.4 = 0.75. At q = 0.6, a is common but below the point. An a copy experiences 1.06 and an A copy 1.12, so a falls.

The key's example is a chromosome rearrangement. Heterozygotes pair a normal and a rearranged chromosome in meiosis and make many unbalanced gametes, while both homozygotes pair normally. A new rearrangement is usually lost, and when one takes over it becomes fixed.

Quick quiz

Under heterozygote disadvantage, the equilibrium is called unstable. What does that mean?

Problem Set 12 question 9 says frequencies "always evolve away from the equilibrium point instead of towards it." One allele fixes.

Quick quiz

Harder. Absolute fitnesses of AA, Aa and aa are 6, 4 and 5. Hardy-Weinberg, q = freq(a) = 0.6. What happens to a over many generations?

The heterozygote is lowest, so divide by it, 1.5, 1 and 1.25. In the Problem Set 12 question 9 form, s1 = 0.5 and s2 = 0.25, and the unstable point is q = 0.5 ÷ 0.75 = 0.667. q = 0.6 is below it. Check directly. An a copy experiences 0.6 × 5 + 0.4 × 4 = 4.6, and an A copy 0.4 × 6 + 0.6 × 4 = 4.8, so A does better and a falls, then keeps falling. The point is unstable, so nothing stays there.

Step 49. The honest limits

The model assumed constant fitnesses. If malaria is controlled, s1 shrinks and q̂ with it. With no malaria s1 = 0, q̂ = 0, and HbS is under purifying selection, as for people of African descent in the United States. Medical care that lets HbS/HbS people have children lowers s2, which raises q̂.

Quick quiz

If treatment raised the relative fitness of HbS/HbS from 0 to 0.5, while s1 stayed 0.121, what would the new q̂ be?

Now s2 = 0.5. q̂ = 0.121/(0.121 + 0.5) = 0.195. Making the homozygote less harmful lets the allele settle at a higher frequency.

The exam procedure

  1. Read what was measured. Survival, fecundity or both. Fitness is viability times fecundity, and one part is enough only if the other is equal across genotypes. Step 7
  2. Ask who selection acts on. If you are given only q and told Hardy-Weinberg, use p², 2pq and q². If selection acts on the individuals counted, use the counted frequencies in general equation #1. If the question asks for p², 2pq and q², it means the next generation's zygotes, so build them from the counted allele frequency. If you only have phenotype counts with a dominant allele, assume Hardy-Weinberg. Counted, mixed or inbred frequencies rule out average excess and the special models. Step 11 and Step 26
  3. Get starting allele frequencies by counting alleles. Use a square root only if Hardy-Weinberg is stated or assumed. Step 11
  4. Convert to relative fitness, W = w/wmax, and s = 1 − W. Step 18
  5. Read the fitness pattern. Heterozygote equal to the best homozygote, selection against the recessive. Heterozygote equal to the worst homozygote, selection against the dominant. Heterozygote above both, heterozygote advantage. Anything else, no special model. Step 38
  6. Name q as the allele selected against, never automatically the recessive. For heterozygote advantage, s2 goes with the q homozygote. Step 33
  7. For general equation #1, find W̄, then q′ from the two genotypes containing q, with the heterozygote halved. Check p′ + q′ = 1. Step 13
  8. For a special model, compute top and bottom separately, then divide. In those two models Δq must be negative. q′ = q + Δq. Step 35
  9. For heterozygote advantage, q̂ = s1/(s1 + s2). Check that the allele with the more harmful homozygote came out rarer. Step 46
  10. After selection, never use 2pq or a square root. Genotype frequencies are contributions divided by W̄. Step 20
